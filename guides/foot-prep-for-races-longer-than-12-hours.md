## Why 12 hours is the line

Under about 50 miles you're usually under 12 hours, and most people — me included — can absorb a foot mistake in that window. Past 12 hours, mistakes compound. That's the threshold where this stops being optional maintenance and starts being race strategy.

Some of what follows is specific to my shoes, and some of it isn't. Shoe-dependent — recalibrate for your own: the 50–200 mile race window, the 48-hour compression rule, the worn-first/new-second swap order, and which spots need lube. Shoe-independent — applies regardless: shaving calluses down ahead of time, the lubricant strategy for dry versus wet, fresh socks at every shoe change, compression socks, and recovery slides before and after. If neither Altra nor Topo is your shoe, most of this still applies — the hot spots just move.

## The race that created this protocol

For a long time my foot prep was nothing special. At the Georgia Jewel 100 — a hot, humid day on the Pinhoti trail in north Georgia, run as a 50-mile out-and-back — I paid for that. I had two pairs of an early version of the Altra Olympus, but I was trying new insoles, and I hadn't run anything of significant length in that combination. Zero blister prep. I'd blistered in races before, but never this bad.

In heat and humidity my sweat rate is high enough that my socks get wet from sweat running down my legs — so my feet were soaked in a race with no rain. Wet feet, plus getting very dehydrated, plus zero prep: the perfect conditions for my feet to be as bad as they could be. By the halfway point the soles of both feet had blistered behind the toes. I ran the last 40 miles on large blisters on both feet, fortunate they didn't pop early. On one foot they popped toward the end.

That race is when I started applying lubricant to my feet. Since then, blisters are rare — and when they happen, it's something rubbing in an odd way, not the entire forefoot. It's also where the 50-mile rule below comes from: the lemon wasn't the shoe itself, it was the untested shoe-plus-insole combination.

## Shoe mileage: over 50, under 200

Most manufacturers say replace at 300–400 miles. My race window is narrower on both ends.

**Under 50 miles, you haven't detected a lemon yet.** Same model, same size, and one pair can still rub. Fifty miles is enough to find out whether *this specific pair* — insoles included — has a bad spot. It's not about break-in; it's about detection.

**Over 200 miles, they're getting worn out.** Fine for training. Not what I want under me at hour 14.

## The 48-hour compression rule

Never race in shoes you've run more than 3–4 miles in during the previous 48 hours. Shoes compress under load and recover with rest, and different brands recover differently.

Here's how I know the recovery effect is real: I've logged hundreds of miles past the manufacturer's recommendation on training shoes by keeping 3–4 pairs in rotation and never running consecutive days in the same pair. That rotation only works *because* foam recovers with rest — which is the same reason a shoe you ran hard in yesterday isn't the shoe you want on race morning. But training tolerance is not race tolerance: a 300-mile shoe is fine for a training run. It is not fine for a 100.

## Changing shoes mid-race

Change shoes for long events. Sometimes switching to a *different type* of shoe helps, not just a fresh pair of the same.

If you're carrying two pairs — one newer, one more worn — **start worn, finish new.** The reason is honest and it isn't biomechanics: putting worn shoes on trashed feet late in a race is demoralizing, and fresh cushion buys you a real hour or two exactly when you need it. The morale effect is not a small thing at hour 15.

This isn't theory from one lucky day. It's what I've done at Cruel Jewel 100, UTMB, Pinhoti 100 several times over, and No Business 100.

Fresh socks at every shoe change. It always feels great.

## Socks

The requirement is a spec, not a brand: **compression, zero cotton.** I've used many different types, and any brand that meets the spec works. Some compression socks have a bit more padding, and that's nice — but the extra cost doesn't justify it. On price: I started with expensive compression socks, then tried no-name brands and got the same performance. The difference is durability — the cheap ones usually last one or two races. ## Skin

Corns and calluses shaved down ahead of time so they aren't thick.

**Dry conditions:** Sports Shield rolled onto every potential hot spot and between the toes. **Wet conditions:** Sports Shield first, then cover the *entire* foot in Vaseline — it keeps water from soaking into the skin. In an all-day rain, plan to reapply mid-race.

The wet protocol has a specific origin, and it isn't mine. At the Chattanooga 100 in 2018 it rained most of the race, and everyone's feet were soaked the entire way. Before the race, someone in the community posted their plan on Facebook: cover the feet entirely with Vaseline and reapply when possible throughout the race. At that point I was using lubricant for friction only, with Vaseline in small amounts and rarely reapplied. I followed the posted plan and had great success — no trench foot, no blisters. The protocol came from the community; I've validated it in every wet race since.

What else I tested: Body Glide works but needs reapplication more often. Squirrel's Nut Butter I tried as a trial version — nothing bad happened, but to me it was more like a lotion, and the extra cost made it redundant next to what I already had. So it gets a mention, not a link. Sports Shield is what's actually on my feet on race day, and I earn a commission if you buy it here.

## What I'd tell someone else

Detect lemons before race day (50 miles minimum on the exact pair, insoles included). Don't race in shoes that haven't rested 48 hours. Carry a second pair and save the fresher one for the back half. Shave the calluses, lube by the weather, change socks at every shoe change, and put on the recovery slides before you drive home. And if you're not in my shoes — literally — expect your hot-spot map to be different, and test everything at training distance first.
