I took in about 85 grams of carbohydrate an hour at Leadville on a gut trained for 65. I missed the Winfield cutoff by fifteen minutes. Here's the full data — including the moment my heart rate hit a ceiling and stayed there for eight hours.

Like the [foot-prep guide](/guides/foot-prep-for-races-longer-than-12-hours), this is n=1 and I'll show the work. I bought everything I raced with myself, including the product that didn't work for me, and nobody is paying me to say any of this. The gear links at the end pay me a commission if you buy through them.

## The thesis

Every fueling guide gives you a number. Ninety grams an hour. A hundred and twenty if you're gut-trained. Those numbers come from real research and the research is good.

The research assumes something that isn't true on a mountain: **that if you get it wrong, you can adjust.**

At altitude, on a course with cutoffs, you mostly can't. Your absorptive ceiling is lower than the published figure, the failure is silent for hours after it begins, and the two things that would let you correct — water to dilute with, and time to let your gut recover — are exactly the two things a race like Leadville doesn't reliably hand you.

Here's the race that taught me that.

## What I actually ran

Leadville Trail 100, August 22, 2026. The Willow Fire forced a reroute this year: no Powerline, no Sugarloaf, no Hagerman Pass Road. **99.7 miles with 13,357 feet of gain** — roughly 2,000 feet less climbing than the traditional course.

I want that on the record early. The course was *easier* than a normal Leadville and I still didn't finish. Whatever went wrong, it wasn't the terrain.

My written plan called for 250–300 kcal/hr, which is **62–75 g of carbohydrate per hour.** That's what I'd rehearsed on long runs for months.

What I actually took in was **75–100 g/hr** — call it 85 average — almost entirely as liquid, plus two Neversecond gels and dried mango twice.

**I had never taken in that much on a training run.** Not once. Not at altitude, not in heat, not on flat ground.

So from mile one I ran roughly **20 grams an hour above a ceiling I'd actually tested.** On any day, in any weather. Twenty grams an hour sounds like a rounding error. Over seven hours it's about 140 grams of carbohydrate that has to go somewhere.

Skratch Labs Super High-Carb, two servings in a 32 oz Nalgene. Five drop bags, and I used four of them.

Two things worth noticing.

**My concentration was exactly what the label recommends.** Skratch's own starting point is two scoops in 16 oz — which works out to the identical 10.6%. I didn't over-concentrate the mix. I just drank a lot of it.

That said, 10.6% sits above the roughly 6–8% isotonic range. A hypertonic drink slows gastric emptying and draws water *into* the lumen on its own, independent of any transporter math. That's a real second-order effect, and it's inherent to how high-carb mixes work — they trade tonicity for calorie density on purpose. Worth knowing you're making that trade.

## Where the carbs actually went

Carbohydrate doesn't diffuse across your intestinal wall. Transporter proteins carry it, and there are two doors.

- **SGLT1** carries glucose and anything that hydrolyzes to glucose — maltodextrin, dextrose, cluster dextrin. Saturates near **60 g/hr** in most people.
- **GLUT5** carries fructose. Separate door, worth roughly another **30–40 g/hr.**

Published 90–120 g/hr figures are only reachable by running both doors in parallel, at a glucose-to-fructose ratio somewhere between 2:1 and 1:0.8.

Now the label. Super High-Carb is Cluster Dextrin plus fructose, and Skratch is upfront about the design: glucose-based carbs plus, in their words, a touch of fructose. **The nutrition panel says 50 g carbohydrate, 4 g sugar.** That sugar is the fructose. So the ratio is about **11.5:1.**

It is a dual-pathway product. It just isn't a *balanced* one. Run the arithmetic at my intake:

| Intake | Glucose-side | Fructose | SGLT1 (~60 g/hr) | GLUT5 (~30–40 g/hr) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 75 g/hr | 69 g | 6.0 g | **over by 9** | 24–34 g/hr idle |
| **85 g/hr** | **78 g** | **6.8 g** | **over by 18** | **23–33 g/hr idle** |
| 100 g/hr | 92 g | 8.0 g | **over by 32** | 22–32 g/hr idle |

That's the whole failure in one table. **I was overloading one transporter by roughly 18 grams an hour while 25 to 33 grams an hour of absorptive capacity sat completely unused.** I was carrying a second door and putting almost nothing through it.

To be fair to Skratch: they don't publish an 11.5:1 claim, they describe it accurately as mostly complex carb with some fructose, and plenty of athletes do fine on it at sane rates. The product did what it says. I used it at a rate I'd never tested, in conditions that lowered my ceiling.

## Why I didn't know for seven hours

**Carbohydrate absorbed in your small intestine produces no gas.** None. Gas is what happens when carbohydrate you *failed* to absorb reaches your colon and gets fermented.

Bloating and gas in an ultra aren't a vague "stomach thing." They are direct physical evidence that a measurable share of what you drank never crossed the gut wall.

I was gassy and stopped for a bathroom at **mile 32** — essentially Half Pipe — around 11am. Upset stomach from mile 37 onward, all the way to Winfield.

Independently, from the watch: my heart rate **stepped down 11 bpm at 11am** and never came back. Hours 4a–11a averaged 126.5. Hours 11a–7p averaged 115.8. Not a drift — a step, then an eight-hour plateau.

Two unrelated data sources, one timestamp.

And on Hope Pass between 2:30 and 4pm — mile 45.4, 12,500 feet, the hardest sustained climb on the course — I was holding **114 to 118 bpm.** Twelve to sixteen beats *lower* than I'd been running on easy rolling terrain before sunrise. Biggest demand of the day, less output than I'd produced nine hours earlier on the flats.

That's what it feels like. Not pain, not a normal bonk where the effort feels impossibly hard. This was asking for power and finding the throttle disconnected.

## The ceiling

I assumed for two days that the heart rate drop might just mean I slowed down. Slowing down lowers heart rate — that's not a mystery, that's arithmetic. So I went looking for the discriminator.

Here it is:

| | Terrain | Pace | Avg HR |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1–2pm** | rolling, near Twin Lakes | **3.55 mi/hr** | **118.0** |
| **4–5pm** | Hope Pass climb, above 11,000 ft | **1.82 mi/hr** | **117.9** |

Half the pace. Vastly more demand — a sustained climb toward 12,500 feet versus rolling valley trail. **Identical heart rate, to a tenth of a beat.**

That's not pacing. Pacing looks like heart rate tracking effort. This is heart rate refusing to track effort *at all* — pinned at roughly 118 for eight hours across terrain that varied by a factor of two in speed and far more in intensity, including a climb I was hiking as hard as I could hike.

Compare it to 7am: 4.46 mi/hr at 130 bpm on easy ground. Nine hours later I could not produce 118 while going all out uphill.

Cardiac output has a ceiling when there's no fuel to oxidize and reduced plasma volume to pump. That's what 118 was. Not a choice.

The step itself is stark in the raw numbers. The hour before Half Pipe: 4.36 mi/hr at 128 bpm. The hour after: 3.00 mi/hr at 116 bpm. **Pace down 31% and heart rate down 11 beats in the same sixty minutes**, on similar rolling terrain — output and capacity falling together, which is decompensation, not restraint.

## The part nobody writes about: I had no way to fix it

The textbook correction is simple. **Dilute, slow down, sip, restart at half rate.** Give the gut water and blood flow and it usually comes back.

I couldn't do it.

### No water to dilute with

I built the drop bags as 32 oz of *premixed* carb, because you can't count on an aid station having what you need. That distrust wasn't paranoid. The 2026 course put a single aid station in the first 20 miles — Carter Summit at mile 10.6, listed in the athlete guide as limited hydration and nutrition — and **when I got there it was out of water.** Next aid was Turquoise Lake Dam at 20.5.

I was carrying four bottles of my own mix, so in the moment it read as a non-event. My system worked. I didn't need them.

Except that's not what happened. **Premixing converts a two-variable system into a one-variable system.** Concentration is locked at packing time. On course the only input I control is volume, and volume moves carb and fluid *together*. To cut carbs I have to drink less — meaning less fluid, at 25% humidity above 10,000 feet. To stay hydrated I have to keep drinking the thing that's hurting me.

The one input that decouples them is plain water. Which is the thing I hadn't packed, because I'd assumed it was the one thing always available.

So the mile 10.6 outage wasn't a near-miss I dodged. It quietly removed the correction I would need four hours later, at a moment I had no way to anticipate.

There's a bitter footnote. Aid stations that day stocked Neversecond's high-carb drink mix and gels, plus Skratch Sport Hydration — a lower-carb, more dilute mix that would have been a reasonable rescue. I never considered switching, because my plan didn't contain the concept of switching.

### Not enough time to spend

The 2026 outbound cutoffs: **Twin Lakes 2:15pm, Hope Pass 4:45pm, Winfield 6:50pm.**

I summited Hope Pass around 4pm. So I had roughly **45 minutes of slack** — not zero, and I want to be accurate about that. But 45 minutes is approximately *one* gut reset, and spending it there means arriving at Winfield with nothing in hand.

As it happened, I reached Winfield at **7:05pm against a 6:50pm cutoff. Fifteen minutes.**

Sit with that number, because it's the cruelest part of the whole day. Across the eight hours after Half Pipe I covered 23.5 miles at 2.93 mi/hr. To arrive fifteen minutes earlier I'd have needed **3.03 mi/hr — three and a half percent faster.** Not a different athlete. Not a different training block. Three and a half percent.

I spent that margin, and much more, in the single hour when my pace fell from 4.36 to 3.00. The race was decided at mile 32 by a fueling error I'd made before the gun went off, and the bill arrived at a gate seven hours later.

So the 45 minutes of slack at Hope Pass was never really slack. Spending it on a gut reset would have ended my day there instead. That's the structural point: **a 30-hour limit with tight intermediate gates means your fueling plan carries almost no error-correction budget.** Whatever you're doing at mile 32, you're largely stuck with. On a race with generous cutoffs you can buy your way out of a fueling mistake with time. At Leadville, time is the currency you're already spending down.

### The ceiling was moving under me

Altitude squeezes from both directions at once.

**Demand rises.** Under hypoxia your respiratory exchange ratio climbs — you shift toward carbohydrate and away from fat, because carbohydrate yields more ATP per liter of oxygen. When oxygen is scarce that's a rational trade. You *need* more carb per hour up high.

**Supply falls.** Hypoxia drives sympathetic activation, shunting blood from the splanchnic circulation to skin and working muscle. Less gut perfusion, slower gastric emptying, lower transporter throughput. That 60 g/hr SGLT1 ceiling isn't a constant — it's a function of how much blood your intestine is getting.

**Heat compounds it.** The 22nd hit 73°F with a 75°F six-hour max against a 66°F August average — about 9 degrees above normal — at 25% relative humidity.

**So does intensity.** I held ~127 bpm for seven straight hours, my hottest hour at 7am. Hard early running suppresses gut blood flow about as well as heat does.

My ceiling was *falling* through the morning at exactly the hours I was pushing intake above anything I'd tested. Somewhere near mile 32 the lines crossed.

## What I ruled out, and how

The conclusion only means something if the alternatives are dead.

| Theory | Evidence against it |
|---|---|
| **Altitude / thin air** | Race day ran 30.60–30.71 inHg — a *high* pressure day. Hope Pass played about 600 ft **lower** than its map elevation. ~2.5% more oxygen than a neutral day. |
| **Under-recovered** | HRV race morning ~39 ms, **highest of the week**. Resting HR 54 vs a 53 baseline. My own sub-30 rule never fired. |
| **Hyponatremia** | Sodium ran 600–800 mg/hr and scaled up with carb load. Bloating and gas are the wrong presentation. |
| **Under-fueled / classic bonk** | I was above plan every hour. If you *absorb* 85 g/hr for ten hours you don't fall apart in hour seven. |
| **Course was too hard** | Reroute cut ~2,000 ft of climbing versus the traditional course. |

Everybody at the turnaround blamed altitude. It's the available narrative when you feel terrible at 12,500 feet. The barometer says otherwise. Altitude was a costume the real problem was wearing.

## The number on the box is somebody else's number

Skratch's own product page carries a testimonial from Adam Peterman — World Mountain and Trail Running Champion — describing 100 g of carbohydrate per bottle and hitting 100 g/hr as relatively easy, with less fatigue and faster recovery.

I have no reason to doubt a word of it. It is almost certainly true *for him*.

It was not true for me, and the page can't tell you which one you are. A world champion's absorption ceiling is a product of years of deliberate gut training on top of an unusual physiology. Printed next to a scoop measurement, it reads like an instruction. It isn't. It's a data point from someone else's body.

**That's the whole argument.** Published numbers are real. They're just not yours until you've tested them.

## Finding your actual ceiling

**1. Read the label properly.** Glucose side: maltodextrin, dextrose, glucose, cluster dextrin / highly branched cyclic dextrin, rice or tapioca syrup solids. Fructose side: fructose, crystalline fructose. Sucrose splits 1:1 and counts on both.

**2. Use the sugar line to find the ratio.** On a cluster-dextrin or maltodextrin product, the "sugars" number is usually your fructose. Total carb minus sugars, divided by sugars, gives you the ratio. Fifty grams carb and four grams sugar is 11.5:1, whatever the front of the bag says about dual pathways.

**3. Do the math on your bottle, not the box.** Grams divided by millilitres times 100 is your concentration. Compare it to the 6–8% isotonic band and know what trade you're making.

**4. Then test it — long, and where you'll race.** Your ceiling is trainable; transporter density calibrates to chronic exposure. But it's discovered, not looked up. The rate you can hold for six-plus hours on similar terrain is your number. Anything higher is a hypothesis.

**5. Race the tested number.** If you've never run it in practice, it isn't yours yet.

## Redesigning the drop bags

The premix solved a real problem and created a worse one. The next version:

- **Concentrate and water carried separately.** Powder pre-measured in a labeled bag, mixed at the aid station. Costs thirty seconds and buys back the concentration variable.
- **Plain water as a mandatory carry**, not a resource I assume the course provides. At least one dedicated bottle at all times.
- **Sodium decoupled from the carb source.** Capsules. If electrolytes ride in the drink, cutting carbs also cuts salt at the worst possible moment.
- **A mix nearer 1:0.8**, so both transporters carry load. I'd rather absorb 70 g/hr than swallow 100 and ferment 30.
- **Intake capped at my tested rate**, indexed down to 60–70 g/hr above 10,000 feet and lower when it's hotter than forecast.
- **A written switch plan.** What's on course, and what I take instead when my own fuel stops working. Mine didn't contain the concept.
- **Gas as a written stop signal.** First bloating: dilute, sip, half rate. Decided in advance, because mile 32 me is not a good decision-maker.
- **Gut-train on the actual mountain.** My Leadville recon runs tested the terrain and not the fuel. Four perfect opportunities, wasted.

## Where the evidence stops

Sea-level carbohydrate absorption is well studied — dual-transporter oxidation rates, SGLT1 saturation, GLUT5 adaptation are solid, replicated work.

**Altitude-specific ceilings are not.** The mechanisms are each individually well supported. Combining them into "60–70 g/hr above 10,000 feet" is extrapolation — a working hypothesis fit to one failure, in one athlete, on one day.

One confound I *can* now resolve, and I'll show the work rather than assert it: the 11 bpm step could in principle have been me choosing to slow down. The pace file rules it out. A heart rate identical to a tenth of a beat at 3.55 mi/hr on rolling ground and 1.82 mi/hr up Hope Pass is a ceiling, not a pacing decision.

What I still can't prove is the exact size of the gap between what I swallowed and what I absorbed. The gas establishes that the gap existed. It doesn't quantify it. Every gram figure in this article is intake, measured at my mouth, not at my bloodstream.

Which is the point. The published numbers are real research conducted somewhere other than where I race. If you race high, you're extrapolating too. Do it deliberately, with your own tested numbers, and build a system that still works when you're wrong.
